There is a big difference between helping a team out once and actually mentoring them. Casual support is great: answering a quick question at a competition, sharing a code snippet online, stopping by their pit to help debug something. But mentoring is something more. It means showing up consistently, understanding what the team is trying to accomplish, and helping them grow over the course of weeks or months. Done well, it is one of the most rewarding things your team can do.

## Casual Support vs. Formal Mentorship

Casual support is low-commitment and high-value. You help when you can, share what you know, and move on. There is nothing wrong with this. It is how most inter-team help happens and it genuinely matters.

Formal mentorship involves a real commitment. You meet regularly (maybe every two weeks), you follow the other team's progress, you set goals with them, and you become a reliable resource they can count on. It requires more time and planning, but the impact is much deeper. When you mentor a team through an entire season, you see them grow in ways that casual help cannot produce.

Before you commit to formal mentorship, be honest with your own team about whether you have the bandwidth. Overcommitting and then disappearing on a team you promised to support is worse than not mentoring at all.

## Setting Up a Mentorship Arrangement

### The Initial Meeting

Start with a conversation, not a lecture. Meet with the team, ideally including their coaches and student leaders, and just listen. What are they struggling with? What are their goals for the season? What have they already tried? What does their schedule look like?

This first meeting should be mostly you asking questions and taking notes. You are there to understand them, not to immediately start telling them what to do.

### Understanding What They Need

Different teams need different things. A brand new rookie team might need help with the fundamentals: setting up their programming environment, understanding the game, learning how to run a meeting. A second-year team might have the basics down but want help improving their engineering notebook or getting more consistent autonomous performance.

Do not assume you know what they need. Ask. Then ask follow-up questions. The better you understand their specific situation, the more useful your mentorship will be.

### Setting Expectations on Time Commitment

Be clear about what you can offer. How often can you meet? For how long? Are you available between meetings for quick questions, and if so, through what channel? Are there weeks during competition season when you will be unavailable?

It is also fair to ask the team what they can commit to. Mentorship works both ways. If they are not going to show up to meetings or follow through on things they agree to try, it is not going to work.

Write this stuff down. A simple shared document with meeting dates and agreed goals helps everyone stay on the same page.

## What to Focus On When Mentoring

The most important thing to understand about mentoring is this: your job is not to do things for them. It is to help them learn to do things themselves.

This sounds obvious, but it is easy to slip into. You see a problem, you know how to fix it, and it is tempting to just fix it. But if you fix it for them, they do not learn anything. Next time the same problem comes up, they will need you again.

Instead, ask questions. "What have you tried so far? What do you think is causing this? What would happen if you changed this variable? What does the documentation say about this?" Guide their thinking rather than replacing it. When they figure something out themselves, even with your help, they own that knowledge in a way they would not if you had just given them the answer.

This approach takes more patience. It is slower. But it produces teams that can actually solve problems independently, which is the whole point.

## Common Areas Where Teams Need Mentorship

### Programming

This is one of the biggest pain points for many teams. Areas where you can add a lot of value: writing clean and organized code, understanding control loops, getting autonomous working reliably, using FTC Dashboard for tuning, and debugging effectively. If your team is strong here, this is probably where you will be most useful.

### Robot Design and Build

Design mentorship works best when you ask questions about trade-offs rather than prescribing solutions. "Why did you choose this mechanism? What are the failure modes? How would you fix it if it broke during a match?" Help them think like engineers.

### Engineering Notebook

Many teams underestimate how much the notebook matters for judging. Help them understand what judges are looking for: documented decisions and their reasoning, iteration, testing data, and reflection. Show them examples of what a strong entry looks like and help them build the habit of documenting as they go, not all at once the night before a tournament.

### Award Preparation

Help them understand which awards they should pursue and how to prepare for them. Walk them through what judges ask and how to answer confidently. Do a practice interview with them. This kind of preparation makes a huge difference for teams that have done the work but do not know how to present it.

## Managing Time Without Burning Out

Mentoring another team takes real time, and your own team has to come first. Here are a few ways to keep the balance.

Schedule mentorship meetings during times that do not overlap with your own team's build sessions or practice. Keep meetings focused and time-boxed. If a topic comes up that would take too long to address in the moment, note it and address it at the next meeting rather than letting a session run over.

Involve multiple members of your team in mentorship activities. Spreading the responsibility means no one person is carrying everything. It also gives more of your own team members a chance to teach, which deepens their own understanding.

If you start to feel overextended, it is okay to scale back. Talk to the team you are mentoring honestly and adjust the arrangement. That conversation is much better than silently disappearing.

## Documenting What You Help With

Keep a record of your mentorship activities. Note the date, what you worked on, and roughly how long it took. This documentation is useful for two reasons.

First, it helps you track the team's progress over the season and plan what to focus on next. Second, it becomes evidence for your own award submissions. Mentoring other teams is exactly the kind of sustained, meaningful outreach that judges value, and having a clear record makes it much easier to write about.

## When You Do Not Know the Answer

This will happen. You will get asked a question you cannot answer. That is completely fine. It is not a problem.

What matters is how you handle it. Do not guess and present it as fact. Instead, say "I am not sure, but let me look that up" or "I do not know off the top of my head. Here is where I would go to find out." Then actually follow through. Modeling good problem-solving habits, including knowing when to look something up, is part of being a good mentor.

You can also connect them with other resources: the FTC documentation, the FTC Discord, your own team's mentor or coach, or another experienced team. Knowing where to find good answers is a skill in itself, and helping them build that skill is just as valuable as answering their question directly.
