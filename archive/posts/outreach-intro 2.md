Outreach is any time your team takes robotics outside of your own meetings and shares it with the world. That could mean rolling your robot into a local school gym and showing third graders how it works, running a workshop where kids get to build something with their hands, or volunteering at a robotics tournament to help younger teams have a good experience. The common thread is that you're using what your team has built and learned to give something back to the community around you.

In FTC, outreach is treated as a serious part of being a competitive team. It's not just something you do to pad your resume. The best teams genuinely enjoy it, and their communities feel that.

## Why Outreach Matters

There are a few different reasons outreach matters, and they're all real.

The first one is impact on your community. A lot of kids have never seen a robot up close. A lot of parents don't know that their kid could learn to build and program one. When you show up at a library event or a school fair and bring your robot, you might be planting a seed that changes what someone thinks is possible for them. That's not small.

The second reason is what it does for your team. Explaining how your robot works to a curious nine-year-old is actually hard. It forces you to understand things at a deeper level. Teams that do a lot of outreach tend to get better at communicating about their work, which makes them better at interviews, better at writing their engineering notebook, and better at working with each other.

The third reason is the Inspire Award. This is the highest award in FTC, and we'll talk more about it in a moment. Your outreach record is a big part of what judges look at when deciding who deserves it.

## Types of Outreach Activities

There's no single template for what outreach has to look like. Different teams find different things that work for them and fit their community. Here are the most common types.

### Robot Demos

This is the most straightforward outreach format. You bring your robot somewhere, set it up, and let people see it run. Good demo events are interactive. You let kids push the joystick and drive the robot themselves. You explain the different systems and why you made the choices you made. You answer questions.

Robot demos work well at community events like local fairs, library events, city festivals, and school open houses. They're relatively easy to set up compared to other formats, and they tend to draw a crowd because robots are genuinely cool to most people who haven't seen one before.

### STEM Workshops

Workshops go deeper than a demo. Instead of just watching, participants actually build or program something. You might run a session where elementary students build simple structures with LEGO or VEX pieces. You might lead a beginner coding activity. Some teams run multi-week after-school programs.

Workshops take more preparation than demos, but they create a stronger experience. When a kid builds something themselves, they remember it. That's the whole point.

### Volunteering at FTC Events

Volunteering at tournaments is a great way to give back to the FTC community specifically. You can serve as a field reset volunteer, help run the queuing area, assist with judging logistics, or mentor teams in the pits.

This kind of outreach is particularly meaningful to judges because it shows that your team cares about the health of the broader program, not just your own success. It also gives your team members a chance to see how events are run from the inside, which is genuinely educational.

### Mentoring Younger Teams

If there are rookie teams in your area, one of the best things you can do is reach out and offer help. This could be a one-time visit where you walk them through your robot and answer questions, or it could turn into an ongoing mentorship where you check in with them throughout the season.

Mentoring younger teams is one of the most impactful things a team can do. It directly grows the FTC community. Judges notice when teams do this because it takes real time and effort and produces results you can point to.

### STEM Fairs and School Events

Many teams participate in local science fairs, school STEM nights, or career days. These events put you in front of a lot of families in a short amount of time. They're usually easy to get into because schools and community organizations are often looking for interesting exhibitors.

## How Outreach Is Judged: The Inspire Award

The Inspire Award is the most prestigious award in FTC. Winning it means the judges believe your team best represents what FTC is all about: strong engineering, great teamwork, gracious professionalism, and a commitment to spreading STEM to others. Outreach is one of the main pillars judges use to evaluate teams for this award.

During your judging session, you'll have a chance to talk about your outreach work. Judges want to hear specific numbers: how many events you ran, how many people you reached, what age groups you worked with, and what lasting impact you created. They also want to see that your outreach was meaningful and intentional, not just a checklist.

What judges are really asking is: did your team actually make a difference in your community? Can you show us evidence of that?

Your engineering notebook should document your outreach throughout the season. That means photos, attendance counts, descriptions of what happened, and reflections on what you learned or would do differently next time.

## Getting Started as a Rookie Team

If this is your first season and outreach feels overwhelming, here's the honest advice: start small and start early.

You don't need to run a 200-person event in your first month. Pick one thing and do it well. A single demo at your local library, done thoughtfully with good planning and genuine engagement, is worth more than four rushed events where your team shows up unprepared.

Contact your school first. Most schools are happy to have a robotics team come talk to students. Reach out to the STEM or science teacher, explain who you are, and ask if you can come in for a class period or an after-school event.

Look for community events already happening around you. Fairs, festivals, and library programming are always looking for exhibitors. Showing up with a working robot almost guarantees you a spot.

Document everything from the beginning. Even if your first event is small, take photos, write down how many people attended, and note what worked and what didn't. That documentation becomes your outreach record for the season, and judges want to see the full picture.

## What Makes Outreach Good

The difference between outreach that matters and outreach that just checks a box comes down to engagement and intention.

Good outreach is interactive. People get to touch things, ask questions, and actually participate. You're not just showing off your robot. You're creating an experience.

Good outreach reaches people who wouldn't otherwise have access to this stuff. Going to an underserved school or a community center in a part of town that doesn't have a lot of STEM resources is more impactful than running an event at a tech company where everyone already knows what a robot is.

Good outreach is consistent. One event is nice. A whole season of events, building relationships with recurring partners, is what actually grows your community. The teams that win Inspire usually have a track record of multiple events across different types of audiences.

Good outreach is something your whole team is involved in. It shouldn't just be the team captain and one or two other people. If your whole team shows up and participates, it means more to the people you're reaching, and it means more to the judges.

Start now. Do it for real. Your community will notice, and so will the judges.
