One of the best things your team can do for outreach is bring your robot out into the real world and show it off. Robot demos are exactly what they sound like: you set up somewhere public, run your robot around, and let people see what your team actually does. It sounds simple, but a good demo takes real planning. A bad demo is just a robot sitting on a table that nobody understands. A great demo gets kids excited about robotics and makes adults ask where they can learn more.

## Where FTC Teams Run Demos

You have a lot of options here. Schools are the most common, especially elementary and middle schools where students haven't seen much robotics yet. Libraries are great too because they often host community events and have the space for it. County fairs, science nights, and community festivals all work well. Some teams have even run demos at halftime during basketball games or other sporting events, which gets you a big crowd fast.

Think about where your target audience is. If you want to inspire younger kids, elementary school STEM nights are perfect. If you want to reach a wider community, a local fair or farmers market booth can get you hundreds of people in a few hours.

## Engaging the Audience

The number one rule of a robot demo is to let people interact with it. Watching a robot drive around is cool for about 30 seconds. Actually driving the robot yourself is something you remember for years.

Let kids take turns at the driver station. Show them the gamepad controls and let them try moving the robot. Keep it simple: "this stick moves it forward and back, this stick turns it left and right." Most kids pick it up in seconds. Parents standing nearby will immediately get more interested when their child lights up.

When you explain what the robot does, skip the technical vocabulary. Instead of saying "our intake mechanism uses compliant wheels to grab game elements," say "this spinning part sucks in the ball so we can score it." Think about how you would explain it to your little sibling or a grandparent who has never seen a robot before. That is the right level.

## Robot Prep

Your robot is going to run for hours during a demo. Battery management is one of the most important things to plan ahead. Fully charge every battery pack you own before the event. Bring at least two or three batteries so you can swap them out without downtime. If you have access to power at the venue, bring a charger and cycle through batteries throughout the day.

Protecting the robot from accidental bumps matters too. During a demo, kids will lean in close, reach out to touch things, and sometimes bump the robot harder than expected. If your robot has sharp metal edges, put foam bumpers or electrical tape over them. Remove any fragile mechanisms that aren't needed for the demo, or at least make sure they're secured. Keep a small toolkit nearby with zip ties, a screwdriver, and extra screws in case something shakes loose mid-event.

## Communication Tips

You are going to have two very different audiences: kids and adults. Adjust your approach for each.

With kids, get down to their level, literally and figuratively. Be enthusiastic. Let them ask questions and actually answer them. Kids often ask things like "how fast does it go?" or "can it fight other robots?" Answer honestly and with energy. If the answer is "not really, but it can grab things and shoot them across the field," make that sound awesome.

With adults, especially parents and teachers, they want to know the bigger picture. They ask things like "how did you learn to build this?" or "where can my kid join a team?" Have answers ready. Know how to explain what FTC is in two or three sentences. Have flyers ready with information about local teams or programs.

Common questions you will get at almost every demo:

- **"How long did it take to build?"** Give a realistic answer. Something like "we started in September and had a working robot by December."
- **"How does it know what to do?"** Explain it simply: "We write code that tells it exactly what to do, step by step."
- **"What grade are you in?"** Always answer this. It helps people connect with the team as real students, not just some faceless group.

## Safety Considerations

Safety at a demo is your responsibility. Keep a clear boundary around the robot's operating area. Use tape on the floor, cones, or have a team member standing at each corner politely asking people to step back. Never run the robot at full speed when people are standing close by. Scale back your speed settings for demos so the robot moves more slowly than it would in competition.

Watch for cables. The tether between the driver station and the robot can be a tripping hazard if it runs across a walking path. Keep it tucked away or run it off to the side. If you are doing a wireless demo, great. One fewer hazard to worry about.

Walk through your robot before the event and look at it from the perspective of a kid reaching out to touch it. Cover anything sharp or pointy.

## Table Setup and Displays

What you bring to a demo matters as much as the robot itself. A bare table with just a robot on it looks unfinished. Here is a solid setup:

- **Team banner or sign** so people know who you are from across the room
- **Photo display** showing your robot being built, your team at competitions, and action shots from matches
- **Flyers** that people can take home, with your team number, social media, and information about how to get involved in FTC
- **Driver station** set up and ready so visitors can actually drive
- **Notebook or portfolio** open to a page so people can see the detail and effort that goes into the engineering process

Keep the table organized. Clutter makes it harder for people to know where to look. Give everything a clear spot and keep it that way throughout the event.

## Capturing the Event for Your Notebook

Your demo counts as outreach for your engineering notebook, but only if you document it well. Before the event, write down the plan: where you are going, what you expect to happen, and what your goals are. During the event, take photos. Get pictures of kids driving the robot, your table setup, and the crowd around you.

After the event, record a rough headcount of how many people you talked to. Write a short reflection: what went well, what you would change, and whether anyone asked about joining a team. This documentation shows judges that your outreach was intentional and that you thought carefully about the impact you were making.

A great demo is not just a fun afternoon. It is a real chance to change how someone thinks about robotics and technology. The more thought you put into how you run it, the bigger that impact will be.
