A robot demo lets people see your robot. A STEM workshop lets people actually learn something. That is the big difference. Workshops are hands-on teaching sessions where you plan an activity, walk students through it, and help them build or code something themselves. It takes more prep than a demo, but it is also way more rewarding. When a kid successfully writes their first block of code or builds a mini mechanism out of cardboard, something clicks. That is the kind of moment that might get someone into robotics for the rest of their life.

## What Makes a Workshop Different from a Demo

At a demo, your team is the focus. People watch you and ask questions. At a workshop, the students are the focus. Your team is there to teach and guide, not to show off. You are running a class, basically, just a more fun and hands-on one. That shift in mindset matters a lot when you're planning and running the event.

## Types of Workshops FTC Teams Run

There are many directions you can take a workshop. Here are some popular ones:

- **Intro to programming.** Use Scratch or a block coding app to teach basic logic. Have students build a simple program that makes a character move or a light change color. This works really well for younger kids who have never coded before.
- **Building simple machines.** Teach students about gears, levers, or pulleys using hands-on models. LEGO kits or popsicle sticks and rubber bands work great here.
- **Design a mechanism.** Give students cardboard, tape, and limited materials and challenge them to build something specific, like a ramp that can guide a ball into a box. This is a classic engineering design activity.
- **Robot driving challenges.** Set up a small obstacle course and let students drive a robot through it. Add a timed challenge to make it competitive and exciting.
- **Wiring basics.** Older students can learn how motors and sensors connect to a control hub. Keep it simple and visual.

Pick the type that fits your audience and the resources you have available.

## Planning Your Curriculum

Good workshops do not happen by accident. Start planning at least two weeks before the event.

**Know your audience first.** Age matters a lot. A workshop for 7-year-olds looks completely different from one for middle schoolers. Younger kids need simpler activities with less reading and more physical doing. Older students can handle more complexity and can work more independently.

**Set a realistic duration.** Most school workshops run 45 minutes to an hour. Community center events might be shorter, around 30 minutes. Plan your activity to fit comfortably within that time, with a few minutes of buffer. If the activity only takes 20 minutes and you have 45, you need to rethink either the activity or your pacing.

**Make a materials list and gather everything early.** Running out of supplies mid-workshop is a nightmare. Count how many students will attend and multiply your materials accordingly. Add 10 to 20 percent extra for mistakes and spills. Pack everything the night before.

## Structure of a Typical Workshop

A good workshop has a clear beginning, middle, and end.

### Opening (5-10 Minutes)

Introduce your team quickly. Tell students who you are, what FTC is, and what they will be doing today. Keep it short. Students want to get to the activity, not sit through a long presentation. Hook them with something cool, like a quick robot demonstration or a surprising fact about technology.

### Hands-On Activity (Main Portion)

This is where the real learning happens. Walk through the activity step by step. Explain what students are doing and why. Check in with every group or individual to make sure nobody is stuck. Encourage trying things even if they might fail. A mechanism that doesn't work the first time teaches more than one that works perfectly on the first try.

Give students clear goals so they know what "done" looks like. A timer on the board or a verbal countdown helps keep energy up and things moving.

### Wrap-Up and Q&A (5-10 Minutes)

Bring everyone back together. Ask a few students to share what they built or what they learned. Celebrate effort, not just results. Then open it up for questions about your team, FTC, or robotics in general. This is a great moment to hand out any flyers you brought. Students and teachers will have questions, and your team should be ready to answer them.

## Managing Groups of Students

Keeping a group of kids on track is one of the hardest parts of running a workshop. A few things that help:

- **Have enough helpers.** For every 5 to 8 students, you want at least one team member actively walking around and helping. If you are a team of six, you can comfortably run a workshop for 30 to 40 students if everyone is actively helping.
- **Use clear, visible instructions.** Write steps on a whiteboard or print out a simple instruction sheet. Students who get confused can refer back to it instead of waiting for help.
- **Move around constantly.** Do not stand at the front of the room. Walk through the groups, check in, encourage, and redirect anyone who is off track.
- **Have an early-finisher activity.** Some students will finish fast. Have a bonus challenge ready so they stay engaged instead of getting bored and distracting others.

## Adapting When Things Go Wrong

No matter how well you plan, something will not go according to plan. A material will run out, an activity will take twice as long as expected, or students will not be interested in what you prepared. That is okay.

If the activity is taking too long, cut a step or simplify it. If students are confused, slow down and demonstrate again rather than just explaining with words. If something is not landing, pivot. Ask the group what they are curious about and follow their energy. The best workshop leaders are flexible.

Always have a backup activity in your back pocket, even just a discussion question or a quick trivia game about robotics. You probably won't need it, but having it ready removes a lot of stress.

## Documenting the Workshop

For your outreach records and engineering notebook, document everything. Before the workshop, write down your goals, the activity plan, and the materials list. During the event, take photos of students working. Get a headcount.

After, write a reflection: how many students attended, what worked well, what you would change, and any feedback you got from teachers or organizers. If a student said something memorable about how the activity made them feel, write that down too. Quotes from participants are great for your portfolio.

Workshops show judges that your team is doing more than just showing up places. You are actively teaching skills and making a difference in your community. The more intentional and well-documented your workshops are, the stronger your outreach story becomes.
