STEM fairs are some of the most valuable outreach events your team can do. Unlike a quick demo at a school, a STEM fair puts your team in a room full of people who are already interested in science and technology. You're surrounded by curious students, engaged parents, and teachers who want to find ways to bring more of this into their classrooms. It's a perfect environment to share what your team does and inspire the next wave of robotics students.

## What a STEM Fair Is

A STEM fair is a public event where schools, companies, clubs, and organizations set up tables or booths to showcase projects related to science, technology, engineering, and math. Think of it like a science fair, but open to teams and community groups too, not just individual student projects. They're usually held at schools, convention centers, or community centers, and they last a few hours. Attendance can range from a few dozen people to several hundred depending on the event.

For FTC teams, STEM fairs are a chance to show off your robot, explain your engineering process, and connect with schools or organizations that might want to host you for future events.

## How FTC Teams Typically Present

Most FTC teams set up a booth with three main elements.

**The robot** is always the centerpiece. People are drawn to it immediately. If the venue allows it, have the robot powered on and ready to move so you can give short live demos throughout the event. Even just driving it in a small circle in your booth area is enough to pull in a crowd.

**A poster board or display** tells the story behind the robot. Include your team name and number, a brief description of the game you're competing in, photos of your robot being built, and diagrams showing how key mechanisms work. Keep the text short and readable. Most people at a STEM fair will spend 30 to 90 seconds at your booth, so your display needs to communicate quickly.

**Your team members** are just as important as the display. Every person at the booth should be ready to talk, explain, and engage. Don't let team members stand around on their phones. You are the human element that makes the booth feel alive.

## What Judges Look For

Many STEM fairs have judges who walk around and evaluate the booths. They're usually looking at a few specific things.

**Communication skills** matter a lot. Can you explain what your robot does clearly and confidently? Can you answer follow-up questions without reading off a poster? Judges are impressed when students talk naturally about their work, not when they recite memorized lines.

**Understanding of the engineering process** is another big one. Judges want to know that your team actually thought through problems and solved them. Be ready to explain a challenge your team faced during the build season and how you fixed it. The more specific you are, the better.

**Community impact** matters too. Judges ask things like "what has your team done for the community?" or "how have you inspired younger students?" Have specific examples ready. A number ("we ran workshops for 50 elementary school kids") is always stronger than a vague answer ("we do outreach sometimes").

## Preparation Tips

**Practice your two-minute pitch.** Every team member at the booth should be able to give a clear, confident summary of your team and robot in about two minutes. Practice it out loud, not just in your head. Time yourself. Get feedback from teammates.

**Know your robot inside and out.** Judges often ask detailed questions. If you only built the drivetrain, know as much as you can about the rest of the robot too. Review your team's engineering notebook before the event so the whole story is fresh in your mind.

**Prepare your visuals carefully.** Your poster board should be easy to read from three feet away. Use big text for headings and short captions under photos. Avoid walls of small text that nobody will stop to read. Diagrams with labels are much better than paragraphs.

**Bring handouts.** Flyers with your team's information, a QR code to your website or social media, and information about how to get started in FTC are all great to have. People will take something physical and actually look at it later.

## Day-of Logistics

Transporting your robot to a fair venue takes some planning. Pack the robot in a secure container or on a cart. Bring all your batteries, your charger, and your driver station. Know ahead of time whether the venue has power outlets you can use. Bring an extension cord just in case.

Give yourself extra time to set up. Most STEM fairs have a setup window before the public arrives. Use all of it. Get your poster up, get the robot ready, organize your table, and do a quick test run of the robot before people start coming in.

Battery management during the event works the same as at a robot demo. Bring multiple batteries and swap them out as they get low. If you are constantly running the robot for demos, batteries drain faster than you expect. Label your batteries so you know which ones have been used.

## Talking to Non-FTC People

Most of the people at a STEM fair have never heard of FTC. You can't assume any background knowledge. Here's how to adjust your language.

Avoid jargon. Instead of "TeleOp period," say "the part of the match where drivers control the robot." Instead of "encoder-based autonomous," say "the robot drives itself using sensors that measure how far the wheels have turned." If someone looks confused, that's your cue to back up and simplify.

Use analogies. They make technical concepts much easier to understand. For example: "The control hub is basically the robot's brain. It takes in information from sensors and tells the motors what to do, kind of like how your brain takes in information and tells your muscles to move."

Ask questions to figure out how much the person already knows. "Have you heard of FTC before?" or "Do you have any experience with robotics?" can help you adjust your pitch so you're not over-explaining to someone who already knows robotics, or going too deep with someone who doesn't.

## Following Up After the Event

The connections you make at a STEM fair can turn into long-term partnerships if you follow up. If a teacher expresses interest in having you visit their school, get their contact information before they walk away. If an organization asks about future collaboration, exchange emails right there.

After the event, send follow-up messages within a few days while the interaction is still fresh. Thank people for stopping by and include a brief note about what you discussed. If you promised to send information about FTC, actually send it.

Keep a running list of organizations and schools your team has connected with through outreach events like STEM fairs. Over time, this list becomes one of your team's most valuable resources. These are the people who already know who you are and are open to working with you again.
