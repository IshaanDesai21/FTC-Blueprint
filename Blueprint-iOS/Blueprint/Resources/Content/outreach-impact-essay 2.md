An impact essay is a written summary of your team's outreach efforts that you submit alongside your award application. For awards like the Inspire Award, judges are not just looking at how well your robot performs - they want to know whether your team is genuinely making a difference in your community. The impact essay is your chance to show them exactly that. A strong essay can be the difference between winning an award and walking away empty-handed, even if your outreach was excellent.

## What Judges Are Actually Looking For

Before you write a single word, you need to understand the judge's perspective. They are reading dozens of submissions. They are looking for a few specific things, and if you do not give it to them clearly, they will move on.

**Genuine impact.** Judges can tell the difference between a team that did outreach to check a box and a team that actually cared. Show them the real effect your work had on people. Did a student say your event inspired them to join a robotics club? Did a school invite you back? Did a parent thank you? Those moments matter.

**Measurable results.** Numbers make impact real. "We helped many people" means nothing. "We reached 250 students across 8 schools and logged 60 volunteer hours" means something. Judges want to see that you tracked your work and can speak to its scale.

**Sustained effort.** One big flashy event near the end of the season does not impress judges. Consistent outreach spread across the whole season does. Show a pattern of effort over many months, with events building on each other.

**Connection to STEM.** FTC's mission is to inspire young people in science, technology, engineering, and math. Your outreach should connect back to that mission. Coding workshops, engineering demos, robotics nights - these are the kinds of activities that resonate.

## How to Structure a Strong Impact Essay

### Start With a Hook

Do not open your essay with "Team 12345 did outreach this season." That is the most boring possible opening. Instead, start with a specific moment or story. Put the reader in the room with you.

Try something like: "When a fifth-grader at Riverside Elementary drove our robot for the first time, she turned to her teacher and said she wanted to be an engineer. That moment is why we do this."

That one sentence does more work than a paragraph of statistics. It makes the judge feel something, and it gives your entire essay a human anchor.

### Give Specific Examples With Numbers

After your hook, walk through your key outreach activities with real details. Name the events. Name the schools or organizations. Give the numbers - how many people you reached, how many hours you spent, how many events you ran.

Do not just list events one after another. Explain the impact of each one. What did people learn? How did they respond? Did anything surprising happen? Did you see something that told you the outreach was actually working?

### What Your Team Learned

This section is often skipped, but judges love it. Talk about how doing outreach changed your team. Did you get better at explaining technical concepts in simple terms? Did you learn to adapt your presentation for different age groups? Did outreach teach you something about your own robot or engineering process?

When you show that your team grew from the experience, it tells judges that this was a genuine learning opportunity - not just a PR exercise.

### Future Plans

End your essay by looking forward. What do you plan to do next season? Are there communities you have not reached yet? Do you want to expand a program that worked well? Showing that your outreach is part of a long-term vision, not just a one-season effort, gives judges confidence that your impact will continue.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

**Vague claims without evidence.** "We inspired many students" and "we made a big impact" are meaningless without numbers and stories to back them up. Every claim should be supported.

**No numbers.** If you do not quantify your outreach, judges have no way to judge its scale. Count everything - people reached, hours spent, events run, schools visited.

**Just listing events.** A lot of teams write essays that read like a calendar: "On October 5th we went to a school. On October 19th we went to a STEM fair." That tells judges what you did, but not why it mattered. Every event needs at least one sentence explaining the impact.

**Writing everything at the last minute.** If you wait until right before the tournament to write your essay, you will not remember the details that make it compelling. This is why keeping a journal throughout the season matters so much - when essay time comes, you already have all your material.

## How to Quantify Your Impact

Go back through your outreach journal and pull your numbers together. Add up total people reached across all events. Total your volunteer hours. Count how many unique schools or organizations you visited. Note any events you repeated, since returning somewhere shows commitment.

Also look for softer evidence of impact. Did a school invite you back? Did an organization reach out to partner with you? Did students from your events join a robotics club? These outcomes are worth mentioning even if they are harder to count.

## Making Your Essay Memorable

The best impact essays are specific, honest, and human. They do not try to sound impressive - they just tell the truth about what the team did and why it mattered. Start with a real moment. Use real numbers. Let your team's genuine enthusiasm come through.

Read your essay out loud before you submit it. If it sounds stiff or robotic, rewrite it in simpler words. Judges are people, and they respond to writing that sounds like a real person talking about something they care about.
