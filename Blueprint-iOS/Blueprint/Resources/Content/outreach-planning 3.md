A well-planned outreach event feels easy to the people attending it. Everything runs smoothly, there's always something to do, and nobody's standing around confused. A poorly planned event feels like exactly what it is: a group of people who showed up without thinking things through. The difference is almost always in how much work happened before the day of the event, not during it.

Planning takes time, but it's the kind of work that pays off every single time. This guide walks through the whole process from booking the event to following up afterward.

## Starting Early: Your Planning Timeline

The biggest mistake teams make is starting too late. You should be thinking about outreach events weeks or even months before they happen.

For larger events like a school STEM night or a community fair, reach out at least four to six weeks in advance. Many schools and community organizations plan their calendars well ahead of time, and if you wait until two weeks before, the spots are often already taken.

For smaller events, like a visit to a single classroom or a library drop-in session, two to three weeks is usually enough. Even then, starting earlier gives you more time to prepare materials and coordinate with your team.

Here's a rough timeline to work from:

**Four to six weeks out:** Identify the event or venue, make initial contact, and confirm the date and logistics. Find out how much space you'll have, how long you'll be there, and what the audience looks like. Knowing that you're presenting to 20 kindergartners is very different from presenting to 150 mixed-age families.

**Two to three weeks out:** Confirm your volunteer list from the team, assign roles, and start preparing any printed materials or activity kits you'll need. If you're running a hands-on activity, test it with a small group first.

**One week out:** Do a full run-through of your setup. Charge batteries, test the robot, check that all your materials are ready. Make sure everyone on the team knows where they're going and when to show up.

**The day before:** Pack everything the night before so you're not scrambling the morning of. Charge batteries one more time. Send a reminder to your volunteers with the arrival time and the address.

## Key Logistics to Figure Out

Every outreach event has the same basic set of logistics you need to work through. Going through these one by one before the event saves you from surprises on the day.

### Venue

Confirm the exact location, including where to park and where to set up. Ask whether there's a loading area or if you need to carry everything through the front entrance. Find out if there are any rules about the space, like not being able to run the robot on certain floors, or needing an electrical outlet for your display.

### Equipment

Your robot is the centerpiece of most events. Make sure you're bringing everything it needs to run: the robot itself, the controller, charged batteries, and a backup battery just in case. If your robot uses a phone or Control Hub for programming, make sure that's charged too.

Beyond the robot, think about what else makes your table engaging. A poster or banner with your team name and number helps people identify you from across the room. Printed handouts about your team or about FTC are useful for parents who want to learn more. If you're doing a hands-on activity, bring all the supplies you need, plus a little extra.

### Volunteers

Think about how many team members you actually need for the event. For a table demo, you generally want at least two or three people: one person driving the robot and explaining how it works, one person engaging with whoever's waiting in line or wandering over, and ideally one person floating and helping wherever needed.

Don't overstaff events either. Twelve people crowded behind a single table looks chaotic and makes it hard for the people you're trying to engage with to know who to talk to. Assign roles clearly so everyone knows what they're doing.

### Transportation

Figure out how the robot is getting to the event. If it's going in someone's car, make sure it can fit safely and won't get damaged in transit. Some robots are bulky and need to be partially disassembled for transport. If that's the case, plan extra time to put it back together on-site.

## Day-of Coordination

Arrive early. You should plan to be set up and ready to go at least 30 minutes before the event starts. This gives you time to handle unexpected problems, like a table that's in the wrong spot, a battery that didn't charge fully, or a display that needs to be rearranged.

Do a quick team huddle once you're set up. Remind everyone of their roles, go over what to say to people who approach the table, and make sure everyone knows what to do if something goes wrong with the robot.

During the event, keep the energy up. It's easy to start strong and fade after an hour. Rotate roles every 30 to 45 minutes so nobody gets tired or bored. The person who was driving the robot can switch to talking to parents while someone else takes over the controller.

Be approachable. Smile, make eye contact, and talk to people at their level. If a little kid walks up, crouch down so you're at eye level with them. If a parent is clearly curious about FTC, take the time to actually explain it. You're not just showing off hardware. You're representing your team and the broader robotics community.

## After the Event

What you do after the event matters almost as much as the event itself.

### Send Thank-You Notes

Email the person who organized or hosted the event and thank them. This takes five minutes and makes a real impression. It also makes it much more likely they'll invite you back or recommend you to other organizers. If your team has a business card or a simple flyer with your contact information, leave one with the organizer so they have a way to reach you.

### Document Everything

This is critical for your Engineering Notebook and for your judging session. As soon as possible after the event, write down:

- The date, location, and name of the event
- How many people attended (ask the organizer if you don't have a count)
- What age groups were represented
- What activities you ran
- How it went, including what worked and what you'd do differently
- Photos from the event (get permission if you're photographing minors)

The more specific you can be, the better. Judges respond to real numbers and real stories. "We demonstrated our robot to approximately 80 students at Lincoln Elementary, including a hands-on driving activity for third and fourth graders" is a lot more compelling than "we did a robot demo at a school."

### Reflect as a Team

Have a short debrief with your team after the event. What went well? What was confusing? Did the activity work the way you expected? This reflection improves your next event and shows judges that your team takes outreach seriously as a learning process, not just a task to complete.

## Building Lasting Relationships

The best outreach programs aren't built on one-off events. They're built on relationships with venues and organizations that invite you back year after year.

After a successful event, follow up with the organizer a few weeks later. Let them know you'd love to come back and ask if there are other opportunities coming up. Many organizations have recurring events, and once you're a known quantity, getting invited back becomes easy.

If you work with a school, offer to stay in touch with the teacher throughout the year. Even sending an update about how your season is going keeps the relationship warm. When families at that school see a news story about FTC or spot your team at a competition, they'll feel a connection to you.

These repeat relationships are what judges find most impressive. Anyone can show up once. Building something ongoing takes real commitment, and it's the kind of commitment that makes a real difference in your community.
