Keeping an outreach journal might feel like extra homework, but it is one of the most useful habits your team can build. Judges cannot give you credit for things they cannot see. If your team spent 40 hours teaching kids to code, ran a robotics demo at three schools, and volunteered at a STEM fair, but you have no record of any of it, that work is invisible on your award submissions. A good journal fixes that. It also helps you look back at what worked, what flopped, and how your team grew over the season.

## Why Documentation Matters

When judges evaluate teams for awards like the Inspire Award or the Connect Award, they are looking for proof - not just a list of things you claim you did, but real evidence of consistent, meaningful outreach. A well-kept journal gives you that proof. It shows a pattern of effort over time, not just one big event thrown together before the tournament.

Beyond awards, the journal helps your team reflect. After a demo at a school, it is easy to say "that went well" and move on. But if you write down what actually happened - what questions kids asked, what confused them, what made them light up - you will be a better presenter next time. Future team members can also read your journal and build on what past members learned.

## What to Record for Each Event

Every time your team does something outreach-related, write an entry. Here is what to include for each one.

### Basic Info

Write down the date, the location, and the type of event. Was it a classroom demo, a community fair, a workshop you hosted, or something else? This basic info seems obvious, but you will forget the details fast if you do not write them down right away.

### Audience and Team Members

Note how many people attended or were reached by your activity. This is part of how you calculate your total impact later. Also list which team members were present. This matters because it shows your whole team is involved in outreach, not just one or two people carrying everything.

### Description of Activities

Write a short paragraph explaining what you actually did. Did you let kids drive the robot? Did you walk them through how a servo works? Did you give a presentation about engineering careers? Be specific. "We did a robot demo" tells judges almost nothing. "We ran a hands-on station where students programmed a Scratch-based obstacle course and then watched a real FTC robot navigate a similar path" tells a story.

### What Went Well

Be honest here. What parts of the event felt smooth and effective? Did a certain explanation click with the audience? Did your team work well together setting up? These are things worth repeating.

### What to Improve

This section is just as important. Did kids lose interest during a long explanation? Was your table layout confusing? Did you run out of handouts? Write it down so you can fix it next time. The teams that grow fastest are the ones that treat every event as a learning opportunity.

## Photo Documentation

Photos are powerful. A photo of a kid's face lighting up when the robot moves, or a shot of a packed classroom during your presentation, says more than words sometimes.

A few things to keep in mind. First, always get permission before photographing people, especially children. At schools, check with the teacher or event organizer ahead of time. Some schools have strict policies about photos of students, so ask before you pull out your phone. When in doubt, photograph the setup, the robot, and wide shots of the room rather than close-ups of individual kids.

Organize your photos by event. Store them in folders labeled by date and event name. When you write your journal entry, note which photos go with it. When award season comes, you will have exactly what you need without digging through hundreds of unorganized images.

## Calculating Your Impact

At the end of each semester or season, tally up your numbers. Judges love seeing this kind of data because it shows sustained effort and real reach.

Track total people reached across all events. Count volunteer hours by multiplying team members present by hours spent at each event. Count the number of schools or organizations you visited. Track repeat events too - returning to the same school or community shows commitment, not just a one-time effort.

Create a simple summary. Something like: "This season we ran 12 outreach events, reached over 300 students, logged 85 volunteer hours, and visited 6 different schools." That one sentence carries a lot of weight in an award submission.

## How to Organize Your Journal

There are a few good ways to structure your entries. The most common is chronological - you write each entry as events happen throughout the season. This is easy to maintain and naturally shows your team's growth over time.

You can also organize by event type. Group all school visits together, all community events together, all online or social media content together. This works well if you want to highlight the range of your outreach.

At the end of each semester, write a brief summary. Reflect on what your team accomplished, what you learned about doing outreach effectively, and what you want to do differently next season. This summary becomes the starting point for your award essays.

## Using Your Journal for Award Submissions

When it is time to write award submissions, your journal is your best resource. Instead of trying to remember what happened six months ago, you just look it up. Pull your impact numbers from your tallies. Pull specific stories from your event descriptions. Pull photos from your organized folders.

Strong award essays are built on specific details, and your journal gives you those details. The team that says "we visited Lincoln Elementary twice this semester, reaching 140 third-graders over four hours, and got invited back because the teacher said it was the best STEM activity her class had all year" will always stand out over the team that says "we did outreach at local schools."

Start your journal at your very first outreach event of the season, even if it feels small. Every entry adds up, and by the end of the year you will have something you are genuinely proud to show judges.
